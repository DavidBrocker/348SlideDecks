From Paired to Independent Samples

Visual comparison

Sampling Theory (Recap)

Comparing two unrelated groups

  • Sampling theory lets us test two unrelated groups.

  • Under \(H_0\) the difference between group means is centered on 0. Under \(H_A\) it is centered somewhere else.

Sampling Theory (Recap)

A difference near 0 looks like the null

  • An observed difference like this is easy to produce when \(H_0\) is true.

Sampling Theory (Recap)

A difference beyond the critical value does not

  • Beyond the critical value, the observed difference would be very unusual under \(H_0\), so we reject it.

Inferential Statistics

Applied to independent groups

  • Our goal is still to test whether a difference is likely due to chance.

  • With independent samples, chance variation now includes differences between individuals in both groups.

Group Variance

Within and between

To compare the means we need to know:

  • Between-group variance: how much the answers from the experimental group differ from the control group.

  • Within-group variance: how much the answers of all participants differ from one another.

Independent-Samples t-Test

Example

The experimental group drinks caffeinated coffee. The control group drinks decaf. I ask both groups to complete a survey to measure their happiness on a scale of 1 to 10.

  • The experimental group has a mean happiness score of 8.

  • The control group has a mean happiness score of 6.

Is the group that drank coffee significantly happier than the control group?

Formulas

Theoretical and computational

Inferential statistics have both theoretical and computational formulas:

Theoretical

The formula if we actually knew the population parameters (\(\sigma\)).

Computational

The formula for estimating the test statistic based solely on sample data.

Theoretical Formula

If we knew the population standard deviation

\[z = \frac{\color{red}{\bar{x}_1} - \color{orange}{\bar{x}_2}}{\color{grey}{\sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}}\]

  • Sample mean, group 1

  • Sample mean, group 2

  • Standard error of the difference, using the population \(\sigma\) (assumed equal in both groups)

Computational Formula

Estimating from the samples (pooled t-test)

\[t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\color{darkblue}{s_p^2}\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} \qquad s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]

  • \(s_p^2\) = pooled variance, a weighted average of the two group variances.

  • \(df = n_1 + n_2 - 2\)

Computational Formula

When the variances are not equal (Welch’s t-test)

\[t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}\]

  • No pooling: each group’s variance keeps its own weight, and df is adjusted (it is usually not a whole number).

  • This is what t.test() does by default in R. Adding var.equal = TRUE gives the pooled version above.

Coffee Example

Manual

Pooled variance and standard error

\(s_p^2 = \dfrac{(10-1)(1)^2 + (10-1)(1)^2}{10 + 10 - 2} = 1.00\) and \(SE = \sqrt{1.00\left(\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{10}\right)} = 0.45\)

Compute t

\(t = \dfrac{8 - 6}{0.45} = 4.47\)

Decide

\(df = 10 + 10 - 2 = 18\). At \(\alpha = .05\) (two-tailed), \(t_{critical} = \pm 2.101\). Since \(4.47 > 2.101\), we reject \(H_0\).

Independent-Samples t-Test

Assumptions

Assumptions

  1. DV is continuous (interval or ratio scale).
  2. Observations are independent, and the data are randomly sampled. Each person appears in only one group.
  3. Normality: each group’s distribution is approximately normal (or the groups are large enough for the CLT).
  4. Homogeneity of variance: variances are similar across groups (tested with Levene’s Test).

Interpreting Levene’s Test

  • Levene’s Test tells us whether the assumption of equal variances holds.

    • If \(p > .05\) → use the top row (“Equal variances assumed”)

    • If \(p < .05\) → use the bottom row (“Equal variances not assumed”)

  • Normality: each group’s distribution should be approximately normal.

Independent-Samples t-Test

Homogeneity of variance

If Levene’s Test is significant, use the t value from the second row and its corresponding significance.

  • t is the test statistic.

  • Sig. (2-tailed) is the p-value.

Why Do We Care?

We need Levene’s Test because if the variance of the experimental group is not equal to the variance of the control group, pooling the two variances is not appropriate. We switch to the unpooled (Welch) version of the test, which does not assume equal variances.

  • A significant Levene’s Test means a significant difference in the variances.

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Design

50 undergraduate students from XYZ University were randomly assigned to one of two groups. One group received a small piece of chocolate (70% dark cocoa) to consume before completing a 10-minute concentration task. The other group received no chocolate. The task measured sustained attention and was scored on a 0-20 scale.

  • IV: chocolate (yes vs. no)

  • DV: concentration task score

  • Design: between-subjects, randomized

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Hypotheses

Null hypothesis \(H_0\)

Concentration is the same with and without chocolate.

\(\mu_\text{chocolate} = \mu_\text{no chocolate}\)

Alternative hypothesis \(H_A\)

Concentration differs between the groups.

\(\mu_\text{chocolate} \neq \mu_\text{no chocolate}\)

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Data and results

Difference t df p CI[ll,uu] d
2.39 3.71 48 <.001 [1.10, 3.69] 1.05

Chocolate: \(M = 15.38\), \(SD = 2.61\). No chocolate: \(M = 12.98\), \(SD = 1.89\).

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Check the assumption

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Run the test

# Pooled (equal variances) t-test
t.test(concentration ~ group, data = choco, var.equal = TRUE)

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Decision, effect size, and report

Decide

\(t(48) = 3.71\), \(p\) < .001. Since \(p < .05\), we reject \(H_0\).

Effect size

\(d = \dfrac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p} = \dfrac{2.39}{2.28} = 1.05\) (a large effect)

An independent-samples t-test showed that students who ate chocolate (M = 15.38, SD = 2.61) scored significantly higher on the concentration task than students who did not (M = 12.98, SD = 1.89), t(48) = 3.71, p < .001, 95% CI [1.10, 3.69], d = 1.05.

Does instrumental music improve studying?

Design

Design Summary
40 students participated in a study examining how background music affects studying. Participants were randomly assigned to study a short academic text under one of two conditions: instrumental music or silence. After 15 minutes of study time, they completed a 10-item multiple-choice quiz.

  • IV: study condition (instrumental music vs. silence)

  • DV: quiz performance (number of correct answers out of 10)

  • Design: between-subjects, randomized

Does instrumental music improve studying?

Hypotheses

Null hypothesis \(H_0\)

Quiz performance is the same with music and in silence.

\(\mu_\text{music} = \mu_\text{silence}\)

Alternative hypothesis \(H_A\)

Quiz performance differs between the conditions.

\(\mu_\text{music} \neq \mu_\text{silence}\)

Does instrumental music improve studying?

Data and results

0.60 1.73 38 0.091 [-0.10, 1.30] 0.55

Music: \(M = 7.65\), \(SD = 1.08\). Silence: \(M = 7.05\), \(SD = 1.11\).

Decision, effect size, and report

Decide

\(t(38) = 1.73\), \(p\) = .091. Since \(p > .05\), we fail to reject \(H_0\).

Effect size

\(d = \dfrac{0.60}{1.09} = 0.55\) (a medium effect)

An independent-samples t-test showed that students who studied with music (M = 7.65, SD = 1.08) did not differ significantly on the quiz from students who studied in silence (M = 7.05, SD = 1.11), t(38) = 1.73, p = .091, 95% CI [-0.10, 1.30], d = 0.55.

Does instrumental music improve studying?

Reading a non-significant result

A medium effect that is not significant is a hint that the study may have been too small to detect it. (If we had predicted a direction before collecting data, the one-tailed \(p\) would be about 0.046. But the direction has to be chosen before you see the result.)

Summary: Independent-Samples t-Test

  • Compares two independent groups.

  • Tests whether the difference in means is significant.

  • Uses pooled variance as the denominator when variances are equal (Welch when they are not).

  • Check assumptions: independence, normality, equal variance (Levene’s Test).

  • Report the test, the means and SDs, the confidence interval, and an effect size (\(d\)).
