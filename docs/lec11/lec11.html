From Paired to Independent Samples

Visual comparison

Sampling Theory (Recap)

Comparing two unrelated groups

  • Sampling theory lets us test two unrelated groups.

  • Under \(H_0\) the difference between group means is centered on 0. Under \(H_A\) it is centered somewhere else.

Sampling Theory (Recap)

A difference near 0 looks like the null

  • An observed difference like this is easy to produce when \(H_0\) is true.

Sampling Theory (Recap)

A difference beyond the critical value does not

  • Beyond the critical value, the observed difference would be very unusual under \(H_0\), so we reject it.

Inferential Statistics

Applied to independent groups

  • Our goal is still to test whether a difference is likely due to chance.

  • With independent samples, chance variation now includes differences between individuals in both groups.

Group Variance

Within and between

To compare the means we need to know:

  • Between-group variance: how much the answers from the experimental group differ from the control group.

  • Within-group variance: how much scores differ from one another inside each group, around that group’s own mean.

Signal and Noise

Same difference, different spread

Signal and Noise

t is signal divided by noise

\[t = \frac{\text{signal}}{\text{noise}} = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{SE}\]

  • In the previous slide, both panels have the same mean difference (\(8 - 6 = 2\)), so the same signal.

  • Little spread: \(t = 4.47\). Lots of spread: \(t = 1.49\). More noise means a smaller \(t\), so a difference is harder to trust.

Independent-Samples t-Test

Example

The experimental group drinks caffeinated coffee. The control group drinks decaf. I ask both groups to complete a survey to measure their happiness on a scale of 1 to 10.

  • The experimental group has a mean happiness score of 8.

  • The control group has a mean happiness score of 6.

Is the group that drank coffee significantly happier than the control group?

A New Sampling Distribution

The difference between two sample means

  • In the one-sample test we asked how far a sample mean falls from \(\mu\).

  • Now there are two sample means, so the thing that bounces around from study to study is their difference, \(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2\).

  • If \(H_0\) is true (both groups come from populations with the same mean), those differences pile up around 0. The test asks how far from 0 ours landed, in standard-error units.

Let’s Watch It Happen

set.seed(seed = 11)

Why the Standard Errors Add

From one mean to a difference of two

\[SE_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad SE_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma^2}{n_2}} = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}\]

  • Each sample mean has its own wobble, \(\sigma^2/n_j\) (the squared standard error).

  • A difference inherits the wobble of both means, and variances add (even when you subtract), so the two squared standard errors add. Then we take the square root.

  • Two noisy means make a noisier difference than either one alone. That is why this denominator is bigger than the one-sample \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

Formulas

Theoretical and computational

Inferential statistics have both theoretical and computational formulas:

Theoretical

The formula if we actually knew the population parameters (\(\sigma\)).

Computational

The formula for estimating the test statistic based solely on sample data.

Theoretical Formula

If we knew the population standard deviation

\[z = \frac{\color{red}{\bar{x}_1} - \color{orange}{\bar{x}_2}}{\color{grey}{\sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}}\]

  • The numerator is really \((\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)\). Under \(H_0\) the second part is \(0\), so it drops out.

  • Sample mean, group 1

  • Sample mean, group 2

  • Standard error of the difference, using the population \(\sigma\) (assumed equal in both groups)

Computational Formula

Estimating from the samples (pooled t-test)

\[t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\color{darkblue}{s_p^2}\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} \qquad s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]

  • \(s_p^2\) = pooled variance, a weighted average of the two group variances.

  • \(df = n_1 + n_2 - 2\)

Computational Formula

When the variances are not equal (Welch’s t-test)

\[t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}\]

  • No pooling: each group’s variance keeps its own weight, and df is adjusted (it is usually not a whole number). Each \(s_j^2 = \dfrac{\sum (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)^2}{n_j - 1}\) is that group’s own variance: squared distances from its own mean, divided by \(n_j - 1\).

  • This is what t.test() does by default in R. Adding var.equal = TRUE gives the pooled version above.

Pooled or Welch: Same Idea

Both add the two squared standard errors

\[\text{Welch: } \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} \qquad\qquad \text{Pooled: } \sqrt{\frac{s_p^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_p^2}{n_2}}, \quad s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]

  • Welch lets each group keep its own variance. Pooling uses one shared \(s_p^2\), a weighted average (bigger groups count more).

  • With equal \(n\) and similar variances the two agree (coffee: identical \(t\)).

  • When variances differ a lot, \(s_p^2\) stops describing either group. Next: what goes wrong.

Where df Comes From

  • The pooled \(s_p^2\) uses two sample means (one per group) to measure spread.

  • Each estimated mean costs one degree of freedom: \((n_1 - 1) + (n_2 - 1) = n_1 + n_2 - 2\).

  • Coffee: \(10 + 10 - 2 = 18\). Welch adjusts df to account for unequal variances, so it is usually not a whole number.

Coffee Example

Manual

Pooled variance and standard error

\(s_p^2 = \dfrac{(10-1)(1)^2 + (10-1)(1)^2}{10 + 10 - 2} = 1.00\) and \(SE = \sqrt{1.00\left(\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{10}\right)} = 0.45\)

Compute t

\(t = \dfrac{8 - 6}{0.45} = 4.47\)

The denominator is \(SE = \sqrt{s_p^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}\), the standard error of the difference between the two means.

Decide

\(df = 10 + 10 - 2 = 18\). At \(\alpha = .05\) (two-tailed), \(t_{critical} = \pm 2.101\). Since \(4.47 > 2.101\), we reject \(H_0\).

Reading the t.test() Output

Where each number comes from

  • t \(= \dfrac{\text{mean difference}}{SE} = \dfrac{8 - 6}{0.45} = 4.47\). The two means are listed at the bottom, the standard error is never printed.

  • df \(= n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 18\). p-value is the area beyond \(\pm t\) in both tails of that \(t\) distribution.

  • 95% CI is the mean difference \(\pm\ t_{critical} \times SE\), an interval for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\).

Independent-Samples t-Test

Assumptions

Assumptions

  1. DV is continuous (interval or ratio scale).
  2. Observations are independent, and the data are randomly sampled. Each person appears in only one group.
  3. Normality: each group’s distribution is approximately normal (or the groups are large enough for the CLT).
  4. Equal variances (homogeneity of variance): the two groups have similar spread. Only the pooled test needs this.

Why Equal Variances?

The pooled test uses one shared variance

  • Pooling says: “both groups are estimating the same \(\sigma^2\), so combine them into one \(s_p^2\).”

  • That is only a fair summary when the groups really do have similar spread.

  • Say a small group is very noisy (\(n = 10\), \(s = 4\)) and a large group is steady (\(n = 40\), \(s = 1\)):

\[SE_{pooled} = 0.69 \qquad SE_{Welch} = \sqrt{\tfrac{4^2}{10} + \tfrac{1^2}{40}} = 1.27\]

  • The pooled version leans on the big, quiet group and understates the noise. A standard error that is too small makes \(t\) too big.

What Goes Wrong

Simulating false alarms

false_alarms.R

set.seed(seed = 348)

# Two groups with the SAME true mean (H0 is true). How often is p < .05?
one_study <- function(n1, sd1, n2, sd2) {
  x <- rnorm(n = n1, mean = 0, sd = sd1)
  y <- rnorm(n = n2, mean = 0, sd = sd2)
  c(pooled = t.test(x = x, y = y, var.equal = TRUE)$p.value,
    welch  = t.test(x = x, y = y, var.equal = FALSE)$p.value)
}

unequal <- replicate(n = 4000, expr = one_study(n1 = 10, sd1 = 4, n2 = 40, sd2 = 1))
equal   <- replicate(n = 4000, expr = one_study(n1 = 25, sd1 = 2, n2 = 25, sd2 = 2))

rbind(unequal_groups = rowMeans(x = unequal < .05),
      equal_groups   = rowMeans(x = equal < .05))
                pooled   welch
unequal_groups 0.30275 0.05275
equal_groups   0.05050 0.05025

What Goes Wrong

The false-alarm rate

  • We want about .05. With unequal spread and unequal \(n\), the pooled test cries wolf 30% of the time. Welch stays near 5%.

  • That is why we check: the pooled test is only trustworthy when the spreads are similar.

Checking Equal Variances

Look first, then test

compare_spread.R

aggregate(x = concentration ~ group, data = choco, FUN = sd)
         group concentration
1    chocolate      2.612729
2 no_chocolate      1.893840

  • Look: are the group SDs in the same ballpark? A rough rule is that the largest SD is no more than about twice the smallest.

  • Test: Levene’s Test asks the same question formally. \(H_0\): the group variances are equal.

Levene’s Test in R

levene_intro.R

library(car)
leveneTest(y = score ~ group, data = noisy)
Levene's Test for Homogeneity of Variance (center = median)
      Df F value    Pr(>F)    
group  1  18.262 9.075e-05 ***
      48                      
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

  • R prints one row of results. There are no “top row / bottom row” versions to choose from. Read the Pr(>F) column: that is the \(p\)-value.

  • \(p < .05\): the variances differ, so do not trust the pooled test. \(p > .05\): no evidence they differ.

What R Does Instead of Two Rows

  • SPSS prints a “pooled” row and a “Welch” row and asks you to pick. In R you choose with one argument:

    • var.equal = FALSE (the default): Welch, no equal-variance assumption

    • var.equal = TRUE: pooled, needs equal variances

  • Traditional route: run Levene’s Test, then pick. If \(p > .05\) pooled is fine, if \(p < .05\) use Welch.

  • Many statisticians now simply always use Welch. When variances are equal it gives nearly the same answer, and when they are not it protects you.

  • In this lecture the examples check Levene’s Test first, then run the pooled test, so you see both steps.

Looking Ahead: Sphericity

The same idea comes back

Here the assumption is equal variances across groups. In a repeated-measures design with three or more conditions, the matching assumption is sphericity: the difference scores between every pair of conditions have similar variance.

The paired \(t\)-test never needed it, because two conditions give only one set of differences.

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Design

50 undergraduate students from XYZ University were randomly assigned to one of two groups. One group received a small piece of chocolate (70% dark cocoa) to consume before completing a 10-minute concentration task. The other group received no chocolate. The task measured sustained attention and was scored on a 0-20 scale.

  • IV: chocolate (yes vs. no)

  • DV: concentration task score

  • Design: between-subjects, randomized

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Hypotheses

Null hypothesis \(H_0\)

Concentration is the same with and without chocolate.

\(\mu_\text{chocolate} = \mu_\text{no chocolate}\)

Alternative hypothesis \(H_A\)

Concentration differs between the groups.

\(\mu_\text{chocolate} \neq \mu_\text{no chocolate}\)

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Data and results

Does chocolate improve concentration?

Decision, effect size, and report

Decide

\(t(48) = 3.71\), \(p\) < .001. Since \(p < .05\), we reject \(H_0\).

Effect size

\(d = \dfrac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p} = \dfrac{2.39}{2.28} = 1.05\) (a large effect), where \(s_p = \sqrt{s_p^2}\) is the square root of the pooled variance

An independent-samples t-test showed that students who ate chocolate (M = 15.38, SD = 2.61) scored significantly higher on the concentration task than students who did not (M = 12.98, SD = 1.89), t(48) = 3.71, p < .001, 95% CI [1.10, 3.69], d = 1.05.

Summary: Independent-Samples t-Test

  • Compares two independent groups.

  • Tests whether the difference in means is significant.

  • Uses pooled variance as the denominator when variances are equal (Welch when they are not).

  • Check assumptions: independence, normality, equal variance (look at the SDs, then Levene’s Test, or just use Welch).

  • Report the test, the means and SDs, the confidence interval, and an effect size (\(d\)).
