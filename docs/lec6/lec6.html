What’s a z-score again?

Z-Score
How many standard deviations a single score (an \(x\)) sits above or below the mean.

Z-scores

Why do we care?

Z-scores re-express the original data points (the \(x\)’s) so we can see, at a glance:

  • How close that \(x\) is to the mean
  • Where it falls in the spread of the distribution
  • How similar this participant is to the rest of the sample
  • How different this participant is from the majority of the sample

Who’s more than 2 SD below the mean?

Z-scores

Going backwards: Imagine that \(\color{orange}{s} = 2\) and \(\color{blue}{\bar{x}} = 25\)

Formula

\[x = \color{blue}{\bar{x}} + z\,\color{orange}{s}\]

  • \(z = 1 \Rightarrow x = 25 + (1)(2) = 27\)
  • \(z = -2 \Rightarrow x = 25 + (-2)(2) = 21\)
  • \(z = 0.5 \Rightarrow x = 25 + (0.5)(2) = 26\)

Calculating a Z-score

Step-by-Step

Two steps

  1. Mean-center the \(x\) values: \(x - \color{blue}{\bar{x}}\) — this makes the mean \(= 0\).
  2. Divide by the standard deviation: \(\dfrac{x - \color{blue}{\bar{x}}}{\color{orange}{s}}\) — this makes the SD \(= 1\).

Calculating Probability

What is the probability of scoring a 94 or higher?

The average grade on the exam was an 86 with a standard deviation of 4.

Calculating Probability

What is the probability of scoring a 94 or higher?

Solution

  • \(z = \dfrac{x-\color{blue}{\bar{x}}}{\color{orange}{s}} = \dfrac{94-86}{4} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2\)
  • Beyond \(z = 2\) is the 2.1% + 0.1% slivers of the curve ≈ 2.3% chance.

Solution

  • \(z = \dfrac{82-86}{4} = \dfrac{-4}{4} = -1\)
  • \(z = \dfrac{90-86}{4} = \dfrac{4}{4} = 1\)
  • Between \(z = -1\) and \(z = 1\): a 68.3% chance of scoring between 82 and 90.

Z-scores and Probability

How does it apply?

In real life, we are usually working with numbers with long decimals rather than nice whole numbers.

  • Because the standard normal curve is standard and normal, we can look up the exact probability for a z-score with decimals.

Z-scores and Probability

The Z-Table

  • Locate the whole number and first decimal along the left side of the table.
  • Locate the second decimal along the top of the table.
  • Where they meet is the proportion of the curve at or below that z.

Z-scores and Probability

The Z-Table

Rule

The z-table gives you the probability of that z-score or less (the area to the left).

  • If you need that z-score or higher, subtract the table value from 1.
    • Esmeralda: .023 chance of failing, so a 1 - .023 = .977 → 97.7% chance of passing.

Z-scores and Probability

Life Satisfaction

In a recent study on world happiness, participants were asked to evaluate their current lives on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 represents the worst possible life and 10 represents the best possible life. The responses were normally distributed, with a mean of 5.4 and a standard deviation of 2.2. Find the probability that a randomly selected study participant’s response was:

  • Less than 4
  • More than 8

Z-scores and Probability

Life Satisfaction: \(\color{blue}{\bar{x}} = 5.4 \mid \color{orange}{s} = 2.2\)

Convert to z first

  • Less than 4: \(z = \dfrac{4-5.4}{2.2} = \dfrac{-1.4}{2.2} = -0.64\)
  • More than 8: \(z = \dfrac{8-5.4}{2.2} = \dfrac{2.6}{2.2} = 1.18\)

  • \(.2611 \times 100 =\) 26.1% of participants rated their life below a 4.

pnorm.R

pnorm(-0.64)                        # .2611 (z-table)
pnorm(4, mean = 5.4, sd = 2.2)      # .2623 (unrounded z)

pnorm (probability) is the z-table: z in, area to the left out. qnorm (quantile) is the reverse: probability in, z out.

MENSA

Scores on an IQ test are approximately normal with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. The organization MENSA, which calls itself the “high IQ society,” requires a score of 130 or higher.

  • What percent of adults would qualify for membership?
    • Find the probability of scoring 130 or higher.
    • \(z = \dfrac{x-\color{blue}{\bar{x}}}{\color{orange}{s}} = \dfrac{130-100}{15} = \dfrac{30}{15} = 2\)

Z-table clarity

The table only ever looks left

Hover over the curve. Shaded = the table value. Left of z: read it directly. Right of z: 1 minus the table value. Between: big area minus small area. The “1 minus” applies to the area, never to \(z\).

Z-scores

“Compared to what?” is everywhere

Newborn weight

Is a 4.3 kg baby big? (\(\mu = 3.4\), \(\sigma = 0.5\))

  • Raw (kg)
  • z-score

\(z = \dfrac{4.3-3.4}{0.5} = 1.8\). Growth charts report exactly this.

Daily screen time

Is 7 hours a lot? (\(\mu = 4.5\), \(\sigma = 1.5\))

  • Raw (hours)
  • z-score

\(z = \dfrac{7-4.5}{1.5} = 1.67\)

Bolt diameter

Is 10.10 mm out of spec? (\(\mu = 10.00\), \(\sigma = 0.05\))

  • Raw (mm)
  • z-score

\(z = \dfrac{10.10-10.00}{0.05} = 2.0\). Quality control flags this.

One-day stock return

Is a \(-2.15\%\) day unusual? (\(\mu = 0.05\%\), \(\sigma = 1.1\%\))

  • Raw (%)
  • z-score

\(z = \dfrac{-2.15-0.05}{1.1} = -2.0\)
