Introduction to Paired-Samples t-Test

What is a paired-samples t-test?

Paired-Samples t-Test
A statistical method for comparing the means of two related sets of scores, such as the same people measured twice.

Purpose of Paired-Samples t-Test

Why use it?

  • To determine whether there is a statistically significant difference between two related group means.

  • Useful in psychology for assessing change due to an intervention, therapy, or experiment.

Connection to the One-Sample t-Test

A paired-samples t-test is a one-sample t-test on the difference scores.

One-sample

\(t = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}\)

Is the sample mean different from a known value?

Paired

\(t = \dfrac{\bar{D} - 0}{s_D / \sqrt{n}}\)

Is the mean difference \(\bar{D}\) different from 0?

Formula for Paired-Samples t-Test

Mathematical representation

Paired-Samples t-Test

\(\large{t = \dfrac{\color{blue}{\bar{D}}}{\color{orange}{s_D}/\sqrt{n}}}\)

  • \(\color{blue}{\bar{D}}\): mean of the differences between paired observations.

  • \(\color{orange}{s_D}\): standard deviation of the differences.

  • \(n\): number of pairs.

  • Degrees of freedom: \(df = n - 1\).

Theoretical Formula Explanation

Understanding each component

Mean difference \(\bar{D}\)
The average of the differences between paired scores.
SD of differences \(s_D\)
How much individual differences vary around the mean difference.
Number of pairs \(n\)
A larger \(n\) shrinks the standard error \(s_D/\sqrt{n}\), so smaller differences can reach significance.

Paired-Samples t-Test

Application in psychology research

A psychologist measures stress levels of a group before and after a mindfulness program. Participants first answer a stress survey, next they participate in a mindfulness exercise. After the exercise, their stress scores are measured again and compared.

Steps

  1. State the null and alternative hypotheses.
  2. Calculate the difference between post- and pre-intervention scores.
  3. Find the mean \(\bar{D}\) and standard deviation \(s_D\) of the differences.
  4. Apply the formula to calculate the t-value and check for significance.

Paired-Samples t-Test

Step 1: hypotheses

Null hypothesis \(H_0\)

There will be no difference in stress before and after the mindfulness exercise.

\(\mu_\text{post} = \mu_\text{pre}\), or equivalently \(\mu_D = 0\)

Alternative hypothesis \(H_A\)

There will be a decrease in stress after the mindfulness exercise.

\(\mu_\text{post} < \mu_\text{pre}\), or equivalently \(\mu_D < 0\)

  • \(D\) is post minus pre.

  • A negative \(D\) means stress went down.

  • From here on, the test only looks at the column of \(D\) values.

Paired-Samples t-Test

Steps 3 and 4: construct the formula (manually)

Mean and SD of the differences

\(\bar{D} = -1.67\) and \(s_D = 1.21\) across \(n = 30\) pairs

Compute t

\(t = \dfrac{\bar{D}}{s_D/\sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{-1.67}{1.21/\sqrt{30}} = -7.53\)

Decide

At \(\alpha = .05\) (two-tailed) and \(df = 29\), \(t_{critical} = \pm 2.045\). Since \(|t| = 7.53 > 2.045\), we reject \(H_0\).

One-Tailed or Two-Tailed?

Matching the test to the hypothesis

  • Our alternative is directional: \(H_A: \mu_\text{post} < \mu_\text{pre}\).

  • t.test() is two-tailed by default. A directional test uses alternative:

one_tailed.R

# post - pre, testing for a DEcrease
t.test(stress$Post, stress$Pre, paired = TRUE, alternative = "less")

  • The t-statistic is the same. The one-tailed p-value is half the two-tailed p-value (when the effect is in the predicted direction), and the confidence interval becomes one-sided.

  • Choose the direction before you see the data. Here the conclusion is the same either way: p < .001.

Why Pairing Helps

The same data, analyzed two ways

-7.53

Paired t-test (uses the pairing)

-3.12

Independent t-test (ignores the pairing)

  • People differ a lot from each other (some start stressed, some start calm). That between-person variability swamps the average change in an independent-samples test.

  • Pairing compares each person to themselves, removing that variability. Because pre and post scores are correlated (r = 0.83), the differences are much less spread out than the raw scores.

Effect Size: Cohen’s d

Why report effect size?

  • Statistical significance (\(p\)) tells us if there is a difference.

  • Effect size tells us how big that difference is.

  • For a paired-samples design, the effect size is \(d_z\):

\(d_z\)

\(d_z = \dfrac{\bar{D}}{s_D}\): the mean difference in units of the standard deviation of the differences.

|d| Interpretation
0.20 Small effect
0.50 Medium effect
0.80 Large effect

Cohen’s d

Mindfulness example

By hand

\(d_z = \dfrac{\bar{D}}{s_D} = \dfrac{-1.67}{1.21} = -1.37\)

The size is \(|d_z| = 1.37\), a large effect. The negative sign just means scores went down.

cohens_d.R

library(rstatix)
stress |>
  pivot_longer(c(Post, Pre),
               names_to = "time", values_to = "score") |>
  cohens_d(score ~ time, paired = TRUE)

rstatix reports the same value: \(d_z = -1.37\).

Reporting Results

APA-style

A paired-samples t-test was conducted to compare stress before and after a mindfulness program. Results showed a significant decrease in stress after the program, t(29) = -7.53, p < .001, 95% CI [-2.12, -1.21], \(d_z\) = -1.37.

  • What kind of test? paired-samples t-test
  • What is being compared? stress before and after the program
  • Is it significant? significant decrease
  • What are the test results? t, df, p, confidence interval, and effect size

Interpreting Results

  • We found a statistically significant decrease in stress (\(p\) < .001).

  • The effect size (\(|d_z| =\) 1.37) indicates a large change.

  • Combining significance and effect size gives us a fuller picture of the data.

Always ask: is this difference not just statistically, but practically meaningful?

Summary of Paired-Samples t-Test

Key takeaways

  • Paired-samples t-tests are ideal for comparing two related sets of scores.

  • They help determine if interventions or conditions produce significant changes.

  • Remember to check the assumptions:

Assumptions

  1. The differences are normally distributed. Check with a histogram, QQ plot, or Shapiro-Wilk test.
  2. The pairs are dependent (related measures). Same participants or matched pairs.
  3. No major outliers in the differences.

Practice

Pre/post stress

A researcher measures 30 participants’ stress before and after a mindfulness program (ex_1: Stress_Pre, Stress_Post). She predicts that stress will be lower afterward.

  1. State \(H_0\) and \(H_A\).
  2. Compute the difference scores (post minus pre).
  3. Run the paired-samples t-test, report \(d_z\), and write an APA sentence.

practice_1.R

set.seed(101)
ex_1 <- tibble(
  ID = 1:30,
  Stress_Pre  = rnorm(30, mean = 20, sd = 4)
) |> mutate(Stress_Post = Stress_Pre - 4 + rnorm(30, 0, 3))
Reveal answer

\(H_0: \mu_D = 0\); \(H_A: \mu_D < 0\). t(29) = -7.87, p < .001, \(d_z\) = -1.44: a significant, large decrease in stress.
