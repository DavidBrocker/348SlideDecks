Sampling Theory

Distribution of sample means

If we can estimate the probability that a sample mean came from a particular distribution, can we also estimate whether it came from the null or the alternative hypothesis?

From Probability to Decision-Making

  • Once we know how likely a sample mean is to occur, we can begin to make decisions about what that likelihood means.

  • We introduced two competing ideas:

Null hypothesis \(H_0\)

No real difference or effect.

Alternative hypothesis \(H_1/H_A\)

A true difference or effect exists.

  • Because samples vary, we can never be 100% certain, only confident to a degree.

Errors in Estimation

Type I and II Error

  • That degree of confidence is defined by \(\alpha\) (alpha), our threshold for error when rejecting \(H_0\).

  • \(\beta\) (beta) is the probability of missing a real effect (failing to reject \(H_0\) when \(H_1\) is true).

Type I Error (\(\alpha\))
Finding a difference that isn’t really there. (False Positive)
Type II Error (\(\beta\))
Missing a difference that actually exists. (False Negative)

The Truth Table

Decision Outcomes in Hypothesis Testing
\[H_0\text{ True}\] \[H_0\text{ False}\]
\[\text{Reject }H_0\] \[\text{Type I Error }(\alpha)\] \[\text{Correct Decision}\\1-\beta\]
\[\text{Fail to Reject } H_0\] \[\text{Correct Decision}\\1-\alpha\] \[\text{Type II Error } (\beta)\]

Alpha and Beta

Seeing the two errors

  • The dashed line is the critical value: beyond it we reject \(H_0\).

  • \(\alpha\) (red): a true null gets rejected (Type I error). \(\beta\) (orange): a real effect is missed (Type II error).

  • Power is \(1 - \beta\): the chance of detecting a real effect.

Why Compare Means?

  • We often want to test whether a sample mean differs from a known population mean.

  • The one-sample t-test helps determine if the sample mean is significantly different from the hypothesized population mean.

Why Not z?

From z to t

  • In lecture 8 we computed \(z = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}\), which needs the population standard deviation \(\sigma\).

  • In practice we almost never know \(\sigma\), so we substitute the sample’s \(s\).

  • But \(s\) changes from sample to sample, especially when \(n\) is small. That is extra uncertainty the normal curve does not account for.

  • So the sampling distribution has heavier tails than the normal curve: the t-distribution. A result must be more extreme to count as unusual.

Why Not z?

Same \(\alpha = .05\), bigger cutoff for small samples

Two-tailed critical values:

1.96

z (\(\sigma\) known)

2.09

t, \(df = 19\) (\(n = 20\))

2.78

t, \(df = 4\) (\(n = 5\))

  • The smaller the sample, the more the t cutoff moves away from the z cutoff. With large \(n\), they are nearly identical.

The t-test

Three types, three cases

One-Sample

\(t=\dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \;|\; \dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}}\)

Paired-Samples

\(t=\dfrac{\bar{D}}{\sigma_D/\sqrt{n}} \;|\; \dfrac{\bar{D}}{s_D/\sqrt{n}}\)

Independent-Samples

\(t = \dfrac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_p^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\)

\(s_p^2 = \dfrac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\)

  • We use the sample SD (\(s\)) to estimate the standard error (\(s/\sqrt{n}\)), since \(\sigma\) is usually unknown. \(s_D\) is the SD of the difference scores and \(s_p^2\) is the pooled variance.

  • The critical value depends on the degrees of freedom: \(df = n - 1\) (one-sample, paired) or \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\) (independent).

The t-distribution

  • The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal curve when df is small, because \(s\) is only an estimate of \(\sigma\).

  • As df grows, the t-distribution approaches the normal curve.

Assumptions of the One-Sample t-Test

Assumptions

  1. The data are continuous (interval or ratio scale).
  2. The sample is randomly selected.
  3. The data are normally distributed (or the sample size is large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to apply).

The One-Sample t-Test Formula

One-Sample t-Test

\(\large{t = \dfrac{\color{blue}{\bar{x}} - \mu}{\color{orange}{s}/\sqrt{n}}}\)

  • \(\color{blue}{\bar{x}}\) = sample mean

  • \(\mu\) = population mean (null hypothesis value)

  • \(\color{orange}{s}/\sqrt{n}\) = the standard error (SE), estimated from the sample: \(s\) is the sample SD and \(n\) the sample size

Hypothesis Testing Steps

Four steps

  1. Set hypotheses: define both the null and alternative.
  2. Collect sample data: use the appropriate sampling method and collect the needed data.
  3. Compute the t-statistic: conduct the correct t-test.
  4. Compare to the critical value: use the t-statistic, sample size (df), and alpha level to determine statistical significance. Equivalently, compare the p-value to \(\alpha\).

Hypothesis Testing Steps

Manual: hypotheses and data

20 students have a mean exam score of 78 (SD = 5). Does the class differ from the national average of 75?

1. Set hypotheses

  • \(H_0\): The average exam score is 75 (\(\mu = 75\)).
  • \(H_A\): The average exam score is not 75 (\(\mu \neq 75\)).

2. Collect sample data

  • \(n = 20\), \(\color{blue}{\bar{x}} = 78\), \(\color{orange}{s} = 5\)

Hypothesis Testing Steps

Manual: test and decide

3. Compute the t-statistic

\(t = \dfrac{78 - 75}{\frac{5}{\sqrt{20}}} = \dfrac{3}{1.118} = 2.68\)

4. Compare to the critical value

  • At \(\alpha = .05\) (two-tailed) and \(df = 19\): \(t_{critical} = \pm 2.093\).
  • Since \(2.68 > 2.093\), we reject \(H_0\).

Hypothesis Testing Steps

Programmatic

one_sample.R

# For Reproducibility
set.seed(20)

n = 20   # sample size
m = 78   # sample mean
mu = 75  # population mean (H0)
s = 5    # sample SD

# Simulate data, then rescale so the sample has exactly mean 78 and SD 5
dat <- rnorm(n)
dat <- m + s * (dat - mean(dat)) / sd(dat)

t.test(dat, mu = mu)

Exam Scores vs. National Average

Hypotheses

A professor wants to test if students in their class scored differently from the national average of 75 on an exam. A sample of 25 students has a mean score of 78 with a standard deviation of 10.

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu = 75\) (no difference)

  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_A\)): \(\mu \neq 75\) (scores differ)

Exam Scores vs. National Average

Manual

Compute t

\(t = \dfrac{\color{blue}{\bar{x}} - \mu}{\frac{\color{orange}{s}}{\sqrt{n}}} = \dfrac{78 - 75}{\frac{10}{\sqrt{25}}} = \dfrac{3}{2} = 1.5\)

Decide

For \(df = 24\) (\(25 - 1\)), the critical value at \(\alpha = .05\) (two-tailed) is \(\pm 2.064\). Since \(1.5 < 2.064\), we fail to reject \(H_0\).

Daily Coffee Consumption

Hypotheses

A coffee company claims that people drink an average of 3 cups of coffee per day. A researcher samples 16 individuals, finding a mean of 2.5 cups and a standard deviation of 1 cup.

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu = 3\)

  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_A\)): \(\mu < 3\) (people drink less)

Daily Coffee Consumption

Manual

Compute t

\(t = \dfrac{2.5 - 3}{1 / \sqrt{16}} = \dfrac{-0.5}{0.25} = -2.0\)

Decide

For \(df = 15\) (\(16 - 1\)), the critical t-value for a one-tailed test at \(\alpha = .05\) is \(-1.753\). Since \(-2.0 < -1.753\), we reject \(H_0\).

Sleep Duration Among College Students

Hypotheses

A health researcher believes college students sleep less than 7 hours per night. A sample of 30 students reports a mean of 6.5 hours with a standard deviation of 1.2 hours.

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu = 7\)

  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_A\)): \(\mu < 7\)

Sleep Duration Among College Students

Manual

Compute t

\(t = \dfrac{6.5 - 7}{1.2 / \sqrt{30}} = \dfrac{-0.5}{0.219} = -2.28\)

Decide

For \(df = 29\), the critical t-value for a one-tailed test at \(\alpha = .05\) is \(-1.699\). Since \(-2.28 < -1.699\), we reject \(H_0\).
