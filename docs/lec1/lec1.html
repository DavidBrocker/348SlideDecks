About the Course

Statistics is the science of collecting, analyzing, and interpreting data to make informed decisions.

  • As a practitioner, your practices need to be informed by evidence-based treatments

  • As a researcher, your claims need to be supported by evidence that can assert a high probability of a relationship or cause and effect

  • Because data is everywhere, even in your Netflix recommendations!

What is Statistics?

Definition and Terminology

Statistics

The field of describing, analyzing, and making inferences based on data either from a sample or a population

Describing

What does the data ‘look’ like?

Analyzing

A mathematical approach to extracting information about the data

Inferences

Applying logical reasoning to predict what might happen in subsequent measurements. Typically, inferences are made from samples and extrapolated into what the population might be ‘expected’ to look like.

Data

Qualitative or Quantitative information that consists of measured or manipulated variables

Sample

A subset of the population of interest

Population

The entire set of interest.

Data Literacy

Why Do I Need It

Statistical and data literacy is useful for being able to make informed decisions — especially when a claim is designed to grab your attention before you’ve had a chance to think it through.

  • If you saw a headline claiming that blueberries could cure cancer, wouldn’t it be helpful to know how to evaluate that claim before sharing it?
  1. How many blueberries, and how “cancer” was even measured?
  2. How long do you have to eat them for?
  3. Is it all types of cancer or a specific one?
  4. Is it truly that blueberries cure cancer, or are they mixed in with something else (diet, exercise, a specific compound at a dose no one eats naturally)?

This is the same skill you’ll use on every “coffee causes/cures ___” headline for the rest of your life.

Too Good to Be True

Wouldn’t it be nice if…

We tend to describe the world the way we wish it were, not always the way it actually is. A few examples of that:

  • Dogs smiled (that “smile” is often a stress signal, not happiness)

  • Birdsong is always beautiful (some of it is territorial alarm-calling)

  • All animals love their children (infanticide is documented across many species)

Our brain is a pattern matching machine

  • We look for information that confirms our thoughts and surroundings

  • We would (often) prefer that things were nice and pretty, but reality is often not so simple

Statistics in Psychology

How Each Profession Uses Data

  • Mental Health Counselors/Social Workers

    • Knowing that a certain percentage of college students suffer from Anxiety, you can improve your outreach and provide material aimed to address and reduce the impact

  • Human Resources/Training and Development

    • If 30% of employees report that there are so few incentives and little to no sense of belonging, you can take actionable steps to address those issues

  • Researchers (Hey That’s What I do!)

    • Hypotheses are only supported when the data suggests that the findings are unlikely to have occurred by chance.

  • Academia (Hey That’s What I do!)

    • If the Average Exam score is an 85 with a Standard Deviation of 20 points, I have reason to believe that the test was either too difficult or the material was not explained well enough

Lecture 1

What is Research?

Research is a systematic process of asking a question about the world and collecting evidence to answer it — as opposed to relying on intuition, anecdote, or “everybody knows that…”

  • It’s systematic: it follows a planned, repeatable process (not a one-off guess).

  • It’s testable: it produces a claim that data could, in principle, prove wrong.

  • It’s shareable: the methods and data are reported clearly enough that someone else could check your work.

Descriptive Research

  • Goal: describe what’s happening, without testing cause and effect

  • Examples: case studies, naturalistic observation, surveys

  • Tells you what is going on, but not why

What Kinds of Research is there?

Correlational Research

  • Goal: measure whether two variables are related to one another

  • You observe variables as they naturally occur — nothing is manipulated

  • Lets you predict one variable from another, but cannot establish that one causes the other

What Kinds of Research is there?

Experimental Research

  • Goal: test whether one variable causes a change in another

  • The researcher manipulates an independent variable (IV) and measures a dependent variable (DV)

  • Participants are (ideally) randomly assigned to conditions, and other variables are controlled

  • This is the only design type that can support a causal claim

Putting the Vocabulary Together

  • Independent Variable (IV): what the researcher manipulates (or the grouping variable)

  • Dependent Variable (DV): what the researcher measures — the outcome

  • Participants, Materials, Procedure, Design: the four building blocks you’ll use to describe how a study was run
